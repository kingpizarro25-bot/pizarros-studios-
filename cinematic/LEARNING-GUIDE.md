# Learning the craft, through this film

You do not need any of this to run the production — the storyboard tells you exactly what to
generate. This is here so that after a few films you are directing rather than following
instructions.

Each section explains one idea using a shot from *From Chaos to Intelligence*, so you can look
at the actual frame while you read it.

---

## 1. How much of the room you see

Camera lenses are described by a number, and that number decides one thing: how much of the room
fits in the picture.

- A **small number** like 24 fits a whole room in. Things at the edges stretch slightly. It feels
  like being *in* the space.
- A **big number** like 100 fits only a coffee cup in. Everything looks flatter and closer
  together. It feels like *studying* something.

**In our film:** Shot 2 uses 24 to show you an entire office and make you feel how much is going
on. Shot 1 uses 100 to show you three pieces of paper. Same room, opposite feelings — and we
never say a word about either.

**The takeaway:** a small number for "here is the situation", a big number for "here is the
thing that matters".

---

## 2. What is sharp, and what is blurry

In any photograph, some distance from the camera is sharp and everything nearer or further is
soft. You can control how much is sharp.

- **Only a little is sharp:** the background melts away. Your eye has nowhere else to go. It
  feels intimate, and slightly claustrophobic.
- **Everything is sharp:** you can look anywhere in the frame. It feels calm and open.

**In our film this carries the whole story.** For the first three quarters, only one thing is
ever sharp — the world is fragmented, so you can only take in one fragment at a time. Then in
Shot 11, everything is sharp at once. The audience relaxes and does not know why.

**The takeaway:** blurriness is not a look, it is a way of controlling where someone is allowed
to look.

---

## 3. Light is direction, not brightness

Beginners think about how much light. Everyone else thinks about **where it comes from**.

- Light from the **front** flattens everything. It is why phone-flash photos look bad and why
  stock photos look like stock photos.
- Light from the **side** creates shadow, and shadow creates shape. Suddenly things look solid.
- Light from **behind** puts a bright edge around the subject and separates it from the
  background without lighting the background at all.

**In our film:** every Act 1 shot is lit hard from one side with nothing filling in the shadows,
which is why half of each frame is simply black. That is deliberate. The black is doing work —
it is the visual form of "information you cannot see".

**The takeaway:** when a shot looks cheap, the problem is almost always that the light is coming
from the front or from everywhere at once.

---

## 4. Let the light have a reason

If a viewer cannot tell where light is coming from, the shot feels fake even if they never
consciously notice.

So we always put a source in the shot, or just outside it: a desk lamp, a monitor, a window, a
strip of light along a wall.

**In our film:** in Shot 3, the phone screen is the light. In Shot 9, an amber lamp on one side
and a screen on the other. Nothing glows for no reason, anywhere in the film.

**The takeaway:** before generating anything, be able to finish the sentence "the light in this
shot comes from ___". If you cannot, the shot will look computer-made.

---

## 5. Moving the camera versus zooming

These feel completely different and are constantly confused.

- **Moving the camera closer** changes your viewpoint — things at the edges shift past you. It
  feels like walking into a room.
- **Zooming** keeps you where you are and just magnifies. It feels like a security camera, and
  it feels cheap.

**In our film:** every "getting closer" is the camera physically travelling. We never zoom.

**The takeaway:** if a move feels flat and uninvolving, it is probably a zoom.

---

## 6. Slower than you think

The most common mistake is moving the camera too fast. Fast movement reads as nervous. Slow
movement reads as confident, expensive, and in control.

**In our film:** every move crosses less than a third of the frame across its whole length. Some
shots do not move at all. When you watch it, the film will feel like it is moving more than it is
— that is the effect you want.

**The takeaway:** generate the move, then ask "could this be half as fast?" The answer is
usually yes.

---

## 7. Cutting, and why rhythm matters

A cut is just the instant one shot becomes another. Where you put it decides the feel.

- Cut **as an action completes** and the film feels smooth and intentional.
- Cut **early, before it completes**, and the film feels urgent.
- Cut **late**, after everything has finished, and it feels heavy and slow.

Shot lengths in this film are not arbitrary: 3.0, 3.5, 3.0 in Act 1 — even and unhurried,
because nothing is happening yet. Then Shot 9, the decision, is only 2.5 seconds. It is the
shortest shot in the film and lands like a punctuation mark.

**The takeaway:** varying shot lengths is how a film gets a pulse. All-equal lengths feel like a
slideshow.

---

## 8. Continuity — the thing nobody notices but everybody feels

Continuity means the world staying consistent from shot to shot. It is the difference between a
film and a collection of clips, and with AI generation it is the single hardest thing to hold on
to, because each generation starts from nothing.

Our defences:
- **The same materials in every shot** — graphite, aluminium, paper, dark glass.
- **The same light direction** through Act 1.
- **One motif that behaves identically every time** — the cyan line always touches a real
  surface, always moves at the same speed.
- **Feeding approved frames back in as reference pictures** for later shots.
- **Shot 11 reusing Shot 2's exact camera position**, so the film feels like it comes home.

**The takeaway:** consistency is not a bonus at the end, it is a decision made at the start and
then defended in every single generation.

---

## 9. Colour that means something

Most videos colour-grade for mood. Ours assigns meaning: cyan is the system, amber is the human.
Once the audience has seen that twice, they read it automatically for the rest of the film — so
when both colours appear at equal strength on a hand in Shot 9, they understand "the system and
the person, together" without a word being said.

**The takeaway:** two colours that mean something will always beat ten that just look nice.

---

## 10. What separates a good shot from a nice-looking one

A nice-looking shot is pretty. A good shot **does a job in the story**. That is why the storyboard
has a "why it exists" line at the top of every shot — before the camera, before the lighting.

If you cannot say what a shot is for, cutting it will improve the film. This is true of every
film ever made, and it is the one habit worth taking away from this whole document.

---

## Where to go next

Watch films with the sound off and ask only: where is the light coming from, and why did they cut
there? Ten minutes of that teaches more than any tutorial. Then watch your own film the same way
and be honest about it.
