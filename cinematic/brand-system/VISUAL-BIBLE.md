# Pizarro Studios — Visual Rulebook

Version 1.0 · Status: active reference

This is the rulebook every Pizarro Studios video follows — the brand film, website videos,
product reveals, social clips, all of it. Its job is consistency. Two videos made a year apart
should look like the same company made them, not like two separate AI sessions.

**Read this before writing a single prompt.**

---

## 0. The one sentence everything serves

> Pizarro Studios builds intelligent systems that change how businesses operate.

If a shot is beautiful but says nothing about intelligence, order, or transformation, it does
not belong in the film.

---

## 1. Where this look comes from

This is not invented from scratch. It is pulled straight out of the existing Pizarro Studios
website (`index.html`), so the videos and the site reinforce each other.

| Colour on the site | Hex | What it means on screen |
|---|---|---|
| Background | `#05070a` | The black. The base of every frame. |
| Panels | `#0a1015` / `#0d151b` | Dark grey surfaces — desks, walls, machine casings. |
| Lines | `#1a2a31` | Edges, seams, the gaps between panels. |
| Cyan | `#55d8e8` | **The system.** Connection, checking, information moving. |
| Dim cyan | `#2e737d` | The same colour switched off or far away. |
| Amber | `#e8934f` | **The person.** Someone present, a decision, warmth. |
| Off-white | `#edf4f5` | Clean light, titles, everything resolved. |
| Grey | `#8a9aa0` | Cold haze, unfinished business. |
| Green | `#8bc99a` | Verified, passed. Use almost never — a confirmation, not decoration. |

The site already commits to near-black, small spaced-out lettering, restrained cyan, and one
warm accent. The videos inherit exactly that.

---

## 2. Colour

**Start with no colour.** About 85% of every frame should be black, grey, and white. Colour is
an event, not a wash over everything.

**Two colours, two meanings — never used just to look nice:**

- **Cyan is the system.** It shows up when something intelligent happens: a connection forming,
  a document being checked, information organising itself. Cyan is always *thin* — a line, an
  edge, the glow of a screen, a bright rim along metal. Never a cyan mist, never a cyan sky.
- **Amber is the human.** It shows up wherever a person is, or a decision gets made: a desk
  lamp, one side of someone's face, the glow of an approval. Amber is always *soft*, and it
  always comes from something you can see in the shot.

**One of them leads per shot.** If cyan is leading, amber is a small distant lamp in the
background, and the other way round. They only both hit full strength once in the whole film —
the moment a person makes the decision (Shot 9). That is the emotional high point, and it is
spent one time only.

**How colour moves through the film:** Act 1 has no cyan at all, just cold grey and a dead
yellow. Cyan arrives as one single line in Shot 4 and grows from there. Act 4 resolves into
clean white. The audience feels intelligence arriving without anyone explaining it.

**Never:** purple-and-blue startup gradients, neon everywhere, rainbow data streams, or that
orange-and-teal look every action movie has.

---

## 3. Light

**One main light, strong shadows, and blacks that stay black.**

- Light comes from something you can point at in the shot — a window, a monitor, a desk lamp, a
  strip of light along a wall. If you cannot say where the light is coming from, it is wrong.
- **Do not brighten the shadows.** Big areas of the frame are allowed to be pure black and read
  as nothing at all. That emptiness is the style.
- **Light objects from behind and the side, not the front.** A bright edge along the side of
  something makes it stand out against the dark without lighting up the whole room. Front
  lighting flattens everything and makes it look like a stock photo.
- **Let the lamp be in the shot.** A visible light source is the main thing separating real
  filming from generic AI glow.
- Thin haze in the air is allowed only when it makes a beam of light visible. Never fog just
  for mood.

How light moves through the film: Act 1 is dim and comes from one hard direction, so
information hides in shadow. In Act 2 the cyan line becomes a light source of its own. Act 3
evens out. Act 4 opens up into the first soft, wide, calm light in the whole film — dawn
through glass.

---

## 4. Camera movement

**Every move needs a reason.** If the shot works better with the camera completely still,
keep it still.

| The move | Plain description | When to use it |
|---|---|---|
| Push in | The camera glides slowly toward the subject | Building focus and tension — Act 1, and the last shot |
| Pull back | The camera glides slowly away | Revealing that a small detail was part of something bigger |
| Track sideways | The camera slides left or right past things | Showing one thing connecting to another across a room |
| Look straight down | Camera directly overhead, pointing at the floor or desk | Order and structure — the best way to show things lining up |
| Rise up | The camera lifts while staying pointed at the subject | Going from one desk to the scale of the whole operation |
| Circle around | The camera walks a slow arc around one object | Only for a single hero object, to show how well made it is |
| Shift the focus | Camera still; sharpness moves from a near thing to a far thing | Moving attention between the person and the system |
| Hold still, close up | No movement at all, very close to a small subject | Detail and texture — let the subject move instead |

**Speed:** every move is slower than feels natural. Aim for the camera to travel less than a
third of the frame across the entire shot.

**Never:** fast whipping pans, drone swoops with no purpose, wobbly handheld pretending to be
energy, movement in every single shot, or a camera move that fights against what the subject
is doing.

**Stillness is a tool.** At least two shots in any film should have a completely still camera.

One thing worth knowing, because it changes how a shot feels: gliding the camera forward is not
the same as zooming. Gliding forward feels like you are walking into the room. Zooming feels
like a security camera. We glide.

## 5. How close, and how wide

Lenses are described by a number. The number decides how much of the room you see and how
close things feel. These numbers go into the generation prompts, so they are worth recognising.

| Number | What you actually see | Use it for |
|---|---|---|
| 100mm close-up | One small thing, sharp; everything behind it melts away | Documents, hands, materials, screen detail |
| 85mm | A person, flattering, clearly separated from the background | The human moment, the decision |
| 50mm | Roughly what your own eyes see — honest, neutral | Screens, mid-distance shots |
| 35mm | A person plus enough of the room to know where they are | Someone inside a working space |
| 24mm | The whole room | Opening shots, showing scale, the calm of Act 4 |
| 18mm | Very wide, slightly dramatic | Rare — only the final big reveal |

**How much is in focus** is a storytelling tool here, not a technical detail. Through Acts 1 to
3, only one thing is sharp at a time and everything else is soft — the world is fragmented, so
you can only take in one piece of it. In Act 4, everything is sharp front to back, because
everything is finally organised. The film literally comes into focus as chaos becomes order.

## 6. Materials

Pizarro Studios spaces are built from real, expensive-feeling, believable stuff:

**Use:** brushed aluminium, dark grey anodised metal, tinted glass, matte black metal, fine
concrete, dark walnut, uncoated paper, machined edges, faint fingerprints on glass, dust
floating in a beam of light, condensation, braided cable.

**Avoid:** chrome on everything, wet-looking plastic, glowing see-through panels, sheets of
glass floating in mid-air, any surface that could not actually be manufactured.

**The test:** could this object be made today and sit on a real desk? If not, cut it.

---

## 7. Spaces

The settings are modern, restrained, and believably real — a serious company's office at an
odd hour, not a spaceship.

- Straight, square lines. Big windows. Visible structure. Lots of empty space.
- Rooms are **under-populated**. Emptiness reads as control.
- Strips of light follow the lines of the building — this is where cyan is allowed to live
  honestly.
- Almost always shot before sunrise or at night. Act 4 is the exception and earns the first
  daylight in the film.
- Rain on glass is allowed and useful — it gives the cyan something to bend through.

---

## 8. Screens and software

This is where most AI company videos fall apart. Ours will not.

- The software shown on screen is **restrained, real, and readable** — the kind of thing
  Pizarro Studios actually builds. Dark background, small spaced-out labels, one accent colour,
  lots of breathing room.
- **No fake sci-fi displays.** No spinning circular scanners, no rotating wireframe globes, no
  rings of meaningless symbols, no green code raining down.
- Text on screen should mostly be **out of focus or too small to read**, so the audience reads
  the *behaviour* instead of the words. When text is readable, it has to be real and true.
- A screen does exactly **one** thing per shot: checking, connecting, or confirming.
- Things move **quickly inside the screen** — about a quarter of a second — while the camera
  moves slowly. That contrast is what makes software feel intelligent.
- Film screens **at an angle, through glass, or as a reflection** more often than flat-on. A
  flat screen recording is not filmmaking.

---

## 9. How things move

**The main idea: the line.**

One thin cyan line is the real main character of the film. It behaves the same way everywhere:

1. It **starts as a point and grows outward** — it never just fades in fully formed.
2. It **travels along real surfaces** — the edge of a desk, a seam in a wall, a cable, a join
   in the architecture. It never floats through empty air.
3. When it reaches something, that thing **becomes ordered** — papers line up, a screen catches
   up, a status resolves.
4. It moves at one steady, confident speed. Never darting, never nervously pulsing.

This single rule is what will make ten separately generated shots feel like one film.

Everything else **settles into place** rather than snapping. Things slow down as they arrive.
Nothing ever bounces. Nothing in a Pizarro film is playful.

---

## 10. Text on screen

- Titles use the same voice as the site: capital letters, widely spaced, small against the
  frame, sitting low or centred with lots of room around them.
- The name is written exactly as the site writes it: `PIZARRO // STUDIOS`, with the second half
  in cyan.
- **Text never does tricks.** It cuts in, or fades in over about a quarter of a second. No
  flying letters, no typewriter effect, no words swooping around.
- Three text moments maximum per film. Ours are: the name, the three-line description, and
  optionally the web address.
- Text sits on black. Never over a busy picture.

---

## 11. Sound

Sound carries the transformation as much as the picture does.

- **Act 1:** sparse, dry, uncomfortable. Isolated noises with no rhythm — paper, distant air
  conditioning, one phone notification. Silence in between.
- **Act 2:** a low steady pulse comes in and starts pulling those noises into time with each
  other.
- **Act 3:** that pulse becomes a bed of sound, layers lock together, a low tone rises
  underneath.
- **Act 4:** everything drops away. One clean resolving chord, then near silence under the name.
- **No voiceover** in the brand film. If the film needs explaining, the film has failed.
- Every visible connection gets a short dry click — a latch, a switch. Never a whoosh, never a
  big rising swell, never the loud brass hit from movie trailers.

---

## 12. The banned list — applies to every single generation

Never generate: glowing AI brains, humanoid robots, floating holograms, blue neon everywhere,
streams of falling code, cyberpunk cities, lens flares, fake futuristic displays, glowing
grids, circuit boards, stock-office people smiling at laptops, generic technology montages,
purple and blue startup gradients, the plastic 3D-render look, warped text, distorted hands or
extra fingers, watermarks, oversaturated colour, smeary blurring.

## 13. The other products

Each product video stays inside this rulebook but changes exactly one thing:

| Product | What changes | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **Veridoc** | Cool white and cyan lead; paper, seals, signatures, glass | It is about documents being genuine |
| **FluencyCoach** | Amber leads; faces, breath, speech | Learning is a human thing |
| **Market Mentor AI** | Harder contrast, charts treated like architecture, city glass at night | Decisions made under pressure |
| **Artist Rollout Planner** | A little more contrast and movement, stage lighting | Creative energy, still restrained |
| **Pizarro Shield** | Almost no colour at all, hard edges | Security should feel severe |

Same camera language, same blacks, same movement rules. Only the accent balance and the subject
change.

## 14. How to use this document

1. Read sections 2 to 5 before writing any prompts.
2. Copy the standard ending from `cinematic/prompts/shot-prompts.md` — it translates this
   rulebook into the wording the generators respond to.
3. Judge every generated frame against section 12 first. If it hits the banned list, throw it
   out and generate again. Do not try to fix it later in the edit.
4. Frames you approve move from `cinematic/generated/` into `cinematic/approved/`.
